“Ye daagh daagh ujala, ye shab-gazida sahar
Vo intezaar tha jiska, ye voh sahar to nahin…”

Subh-E-Azadi, Faiz Ahmad Faiz

There is something almost cruel attached to the feeling of wanting to return to a home that no longer exists, but only in your memories, just out of the reach for you to be able to go back.

In Imtiaz Ali’s ‘Main Vaapas Aaunga’, Sargodha exists in exactly this contradiction. For the 95-year-old Sikh patriarch Ishar Singh Grewal or “Keenu”, Sargodha is not just a city. It is ‘home’, the only thing clear in his desperate desire to revisit the Punjab he was born and grew up in. While his adult sons put it down to his dementia for his sudden aggressive need to reach back to the place that is etched deeply in the boy, he was and the man he was forced to become, the old man seems to refuse to let go of his trickles of memory attached to the landscape of his youth, of familiar roads and fields, of a beloved family lost and above all, of a love that history interrupted. For the world outside his memory, the first half of his 17 belonged to ‘Pakistan’, where now stands a border and a vital air force base for a nation born out of the accumulated political violence of the infamous Partition of 1947.

The film’s central focus remains around Keenu’s promise of, “Main Vaapas Aaunga/I will come back”, which at first sounds like the language of romance. But if looked more closely, it is also the language of displacement and lost identity. It is not only about returning to a lost lover; it is also about trying one last time to seek that young sheltered self of his who still had the privilege of choosing denial over reality to protect his love from the unrest surrounding them. It is not only about fulfilling a promise, a promise that soon turned into a mutated vow that consumed his entire life.

And this is where ‘Main Vaapas Aaunga’ perhaps crosses through the conventions of the typical Partition romance with an optimistic ending into the thresholds of a rather humanistic and desolate rendition of the very real experiences of people belonging to this era.

For the elderly Keenu, played by the stellar Naseeruddin Shah, the Sargodha of his fragmented memories is not quite the place that exists on a contemporary map. The city that Keenu remembers, reflected in the flashbacks of his younger self played oh so earnestly by Vedang Raina, is populated by intimacies and possibilities of a life imagined which had shaped his person, before history rushed by. The city that exists now is beyond a border into hostile territory, technically right beside but politically unreachable.

This distinction matters heavily. Partition created two sovereign states separated by a border still undefined at large. But it also produced millions of people for whom home and homeland suddenly stopped meaning the same thing. According to existing official records from governments of both nations, around 15 million people were displaced during the Partition, becoming refugees in their own homes, unwelcome in territories that had once shared the same colonial space. Therefore, the certainty with which a political map could answer the important question of “Where do you belong?”, is not the level of certainty with which mere human memory could ascertain.

And this is the film’s most silent argument: A state can change the political or geographical status of a place faster than it can replace the emotional anchor it exists as, in the minds of its people. The dichotomy of the film is such that It takes the illness of memory that leaves you lost and disoriented, to bring back that simple sense of belonging. For the elderly Keenu, who was shown in an earlier scene trying to take a car all the way to Sargodha in a last ditch attempt to go back until he was detained at the border, the concept of two separate nations divided by an ink-drawn line no longer matters.

However, there also exists an uncomfortable consequence to this idea which often slips through the cracks across Partition history — if we are romanticising the idea or desire to return to a lost home, we also need to ask if the right to return was ever gender neutral, because the history of Partition clearly tells us the ability to return was never an equal opportunity, especially for women, an idea that the film itself doesn't succeed in articulating entirely as well, even though attempts were made across a few scenes in the truly compassionate storytelling by both Imtiaz Ali and Aarti Bajaj.

The language of Partition often makes displacement feel to be a universal tragedy. People left, families crossed, refugees arrived, homes were abandoned. But beneath this apparently neutral vocabulary was an intensely gendered experience. In focusing on building up this particular historical juncture by bringing in different marginal narratives speaking into the various aspects of the violent drawing of borders, what people often forget is that this was also a time when nationalism and nation building was being written upon and through the very being of women, both literally and metaphorically.

When Punjab was partitioned, it caused the greatest displacement of the human population in the 20th century, with the religious identities of those displaced being the only significant identity marker, since it decided where they could be allowed to live. The migration of the communities across the new borders however were so devastatingly violent that it continues to be compared to the Holocaust due to the number of lives that were lost to it.

Among those who were impacted the most during this traumatizing period of history were mostly Hindu, Sikh and Muslim women. According to scholars like Menon, over 75,000 women were abducted, while several others experienced violence of horrifying degrees such as rape, forced conversions, mutilations, branding with religious symbols and nationalist slogans at the hands of men from the other community, including poisoning, decapitation, immolation and other such grotesque savagery at the hands of their own community as well, as a means to preserve the purity of the religion by avoiding abduction and possible rape or even contact with men from the other community in any form whatsoever. Because women were now symbolic representation of their religious communities. The Partition border was drawn across land, but communal violence treated women's bodies and their literal existence as a separate territory to claim stake at. As noted by Paola Bachhetta, violence against women during such high scale conflicts were essentially committed to not only attempt at ethnic erasure of a particular religious community whose burden of existence stemmed from the women of their community, but also to depict these barbaric acts to be ultimately viewed as ‘feminisation’ of the men related to these women for their inability to be able to protect their women whom they were putting up as the metaphorical symbols of their religious or communal identities. This was however only perpetuating continuous vicious cycles of depravity against both Muslim and non-Muslim women since those attacking them could be of different communities, but the victims and their sufferings remained very much the same, reflected in the historical records of 1947.

Moreover, in order to later cope with these atrocities in an even more twisted manner, the governments of India and Pakistan collectively signed the Inter-Dominion Treaty to reclaim all the women who had been separated from their families during Partition. Hindu and Sikh women in Pakistan were to be “returned” to their communities in India and the Muslim women in India were to go back to Pakistan.

Why was this decision claimed to be ‘twisted’ when it gave the victims an opportunity to be able to return to their families and seek their original identity, one may ask. It was an almost deviant act because this was just another veiled attempt at establishing nationalist control over women’s bodies and their identities as they still continued to remain as mere objects to be possessed for the sake of the society, over the need for their protection because they too were just humans who were victims to a garb of a political move.

This decision quietly reflected how a woman could be rescued from violence and returned to apparent safety, without necessarily being restored to agency. Contrary to how the governments expected the women or their communities to react or behave, hundreds of abducted girls and women would refuse to return because they did not expect their families to support or even accept them back after the violence they had faced. Which proved to be true in several cases as members of these communities would often end the lives of the victims if they did return since they were now believed to be ‘impure’ due to their non-consensual (mostly sexual) involvement with men of the other communities.

This is exactly why the idea of “returning home” is far more complicated a concept in this context, particularly for women, despite the romantic simplicity of the phrase and the male-centric experiences attached to it. As is the central sub-plot of this romantic retelling of a rather outré Partition love story. So, while Director Imtiaz Ali sets out to help the Grewals realise why their patriarch wants one last visit to his hometown in undivided Punjab, now in Pakistan, his narration of the Partition’s horrors and the nostalgia of the lost home belongs exclusively to the men. The women do not get the chance to miss home. Rather they don't have the option to.

The sequence of inhumane violence towards the Grewal women depicted in the film stands out because of how indistinguishable the women end up becoming in the face of such horrors as none is named, except for Keenu’s cousin Phoola, played by the up-and-coming Aliyah Khan, and even she loses her own identity to be remembered in time as Keenu’s sister, another daughter of the Grewals lost to the brutalities of the Partition. These women remain frozen in time, none of them crossing the Radcliffe Line. Their men, who do cross it in search of a new home and safety, who promised to come back for them, spend a lifetime with the trauma of their loss once they get to know of the women’s fate. They do not pass the knowledge down to the next generations, locking away the family's post-Partition experiences and essentially erasing the existence of these women as individuals themselves.

The experiences of these women ended up as footnotes in the background of Ali’s telling of that history, for his protagonist’s longing for one woman, Afsana (played by the transcendent Sharvari), whose grief and pain framed by the Partition also remained unexplored at large, over Keenu's promise of coming back for her, only to realise the world that made his home no longer existed. ‘Main Vaapas Aaunga’ thus is most moving when read not as a film about just crossing a border and the trials that come along with it, but as a film about the political production of belonging. A man’s relationship with his homeland is usually perceived with nostalgia, sacrifice, longing, while a woman’s relationship with homeland has historically been tied to family and community honour, marriage, preservation of her “purity”, anything over her individual self. Hence, “home” is not just a geographical area, but rather a political decision about who is allowed to belong. A decision which hasn't exactly been gender-blind.

As the credits roll with visuals that ends up pulling the film’s historical pain into the present as the screen leaves the Partition behind for a few seconds to turn towards Gaza, another landscape of high scale displacement, grief and brutalities, just in the 21st century. It is an image which forces us to recognize that the question “Where is home?” continues to remain painfully alive still. While Ali risks this unexpected montage turning distinctly separate histories into a universal language of suffering, it also offers the most humane realisation of how politics of displacement refuses to remain in the past. The comparison shouldn't make these experiences identical, rather it should make the audience more attentive towards what each count of history is saying about the human cost of dispossession.
