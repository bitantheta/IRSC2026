## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The Strait of Malacca is one of the most strategically important maritime corridors which links the Indian Ocean with the South China Sea and the broader Pacific region. Geographically located between the Malay Peninsula and the Indonesian island of Sumatra, the Malacca strait is a critical maritime chokepoint for advocating international trade, movement of goods, energy resources, and people. Besides its commercial vitality, the Strait of Malacca underscores substantial geopolitical importance. Major economies including China, Japan, South Korea, and India are dependent on these vital maritime routes. Simultaneously, owing to its narrow geography and high traffic density, it renders vulnerability leading to maritime congestion, environmental risks, piracy, and disruptions to the global supply chain. The role of this Strait is not fundamentally limited to just a shipping route but also acts as a crucial component of the broader Indo-Pacific economic and strategic architecture. With the evolution of global trade patterns, emergence of new sectors of market, and intensification of geopolitics; the governance of the Malacca Strait increasingly prioritises regional cooperation, infrastructural innovation, maritime security, and various adaptive governance measures. Tackling these contemporary developments is pivotal in assessing the overall role of the Malacca Strait as a resilient artery of global trade and commerce.

## A CHOKEPOINT IN TRANSITION

Global trade has undoubtedly entered a new era that has been increasingly shaped by supply chain realignments, contestation with regards to critical resources, and shifting geopolitical alliances. In light of these developments, the role of any maritime chokepoint is analysed through the intersection of connectivity and strategic resilience. As a 550 mile long waterway, the Strait of Malacca is often referred to as the “maritime Silk Road’s throat”, and plays a pivotal role in global seaborne trade. Moreover, the Malacca Strait is bounded by countries like Indonesia, Thailand, Malaysia, and Singapore. Additionally, it yields the shortest sea route from East Asia to West Asia and Europe. The Strait of Malacca facilitates almost 22% of global maritime trade, positioning it as one of the world's most imperative commercial waterways. The strategic significance of this strait becomes more actively pronounced in the energy sector. During the first half of 2025, an approximate 23.2 million barrels of oil per day passed through this strait, constituting around 29% of global maritime oil flows.

Furthermore, around 102,500 vessels transited the Strait in 2025, which indicates an increase compared to almost 94,300 in 2024. This makes the Strait central to both regional and global energy security, particularly in the context of the East Asian Economies. Approximately 75% of China’s seaborne crude oil imports from West Asia and Africa pass through the Malacca strait, hinting at any possible disruption that could be consequential for China’s energy supply and regional markets at large. These factors culminate into a renewed attention directed towards the Strait’s strategic vulnerability amidst the recent instability that characterised the Strait of Hormuz. The Malacca Dilemma, coined by the Chinese President Hu Jintao in 2003 has therefore become embedded in the Chinese security discourse. It highlights the country’s economic and energy dependency on the Strait and the possibility of encountering a blockade to the Chinese Sea Lines of Communication (SLOC). The Malacca Dilemma, therefore, has not merely amplified China's concern regarding maritime security but has also advocated for infrastructure oriented attempts to scale down dependence on the chokepoint.

## ECONOMIC AND POLICY IMPLICATIONS FOR LITTORAL STATES

The Strait of Malacca falls under the jurisdiction of three Littoral States namely, Indonesia, Malaysia, and Singapore who jointly share the responsibilities associated with the maritime and security aspect of the strait. The Strait’s legal regime is supervised through the 1982 United Nations Convention on the Laws of the Sea (UNCLOS), ratified by the three states. With reference to the Convention, a guiding principle for governing the Strait known as “transit passage” grants vessels of all states the freedom of navigation internationally through the waterway, provided that they are subject to the obligations imposed by the Convention. Thus, the economies of the aforementioned states are intrinsically connected with the Strait. Singapore, being home to one of the world’s busiest ports has recaliberated its position as a major maritime and logistical hub. Similarly, Malaysia through Port Klang and Tanjung Pelepas has positioned itself as a pertinent transshipment center, whereas Indonesia has strengthened their maritime identity with the help of initiatives such as the Global Maritime Fulcrum. The Strait of Malacca has played a significant role in calibrating regional policy dynamics particularly in context of port congestion, environmental sustainability, promotion of trade, maritime security and by extension, fostering an adaptive governance landscape. This is further illustrated in the regional cooperative mechanism named as the "Cooperative Mechanism on Safety of Navigation and Environmental Protection in the Straits of Malacca and Singapore” (CM- SOM). This initiative demonstrates the efforts undertaken by littoral states alongside various stakeholders to mitigate shared challenges targeting navigation and environment.

## CHANGING MARITIME TRADE DYNAMICS

Trade Dynamics have also undergone significant change in response to the shift in global production patterns, rapid infrastructural development, and with the transition towards low carbon energy. Three trends have specifically characterised the evolution of trade patterns in the Strait of Malacca. Firstly, maritime traffic has rapidly shifted itself towards containerised cargoes and intermediate goods which reflects the divergence of both global and regional networks. With a calculated approach towards the fragmentation of manufactured goods distributed across various economies, the Strait has established itself as important not only for finished products but also for the transportation of raw materials between major Asian manufacturing sectors. Secondly, China’s Belt and Road Initiative has fostered the Strait’s connectivity with both regional and global trade networks. With the growing investment in developing ports, railways, logistical hubs, maritime corridors, and other essential infrastructure, the Strait’s has reflected upon its imperative role in strengthening East-West trade. Thirdly, Energy Security has constituted a cornerstone of maritime trade. The increasing demand for critical minerals ranging from lithium, cobalt, nickel, and various other rare earth elements have highlighted the strategic significance of Southeast Asia. This remains important in the case of the emerging supply chains sector associated with renewable energy, and advanced manufacturing units. With supply chain diversification, growing maritime safety regulations, evolving regional trade, and changing international demands, the Strait of Malacca continues to function as a connecting strategic linkage between the Indian Ocean and the Pacific Ocean, converging major production sites across Asia. After the 2026 Strait of Hormuz disruption, the question concerns whether existing regional maritime institutions can remain immune to any disruption that occurs in the Malacca without leading to systemic consequences.

## INDIA AND THE STRAIT OF MALACCA

India’s strategic positioning in the Indian Ocean renders it an important interest in the security of the Strait of Malacca. Considered as a part within the broader ambit of the Indo-Pacific strategy, India has reflected on its interest in participating in the Malacca Strait Patrol (MSP) which is a cooperative security arrangement constituted by Indonesia, Malaysia, and Thailand who form the Tiger Cub Economies besides Singapore (one of the four Asian Tiger Economies). An outlook towards India’s strategic interest reveals that its emphasis on the Strait of Malacca is driven by its proximity to the waterway through the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. India has not been able to join MSP despite Singapore’s acknowledgement of the Indian interest. Nevertheless, India continues to strengthen their maritime cooperation with the littoral states through maritime initiatives such as IOS SAGAR and further necessary naval engagements. INS Sunayna initiated port calls including Jakarta, Singapore and Phuket, and this initiative consisted of training in navigation, maritime security, VBSS, firefighting, damage control and associated areas. This suggests that India has increasingly facilitated maritime cooperation, information exchange, interoperability, and promoted maritime domain awareness. This underscores India’s unwavering commitment towards their objective of promoting a “free, open, inclusive, and rules-based Indo-Pacific”. An analysis would highlight how this approach balances both littoral states oriented approaches towards governing the Strait alongside upholding constructive maritime dialogue with external stakeholders. Around 55% of India’s external trade is estimated to transit through this Strait, and inevitably its stability has remained closely tied to India’s economic and maritime security interests.

## WAY FORWARD

The resilience of the Strait of Malacca necessitates a strategic shift from chokepoint security towards a more adaptive and cooperative maritime governance structure. To this effect, littoral states can strengthen maritime domain awareness through AIS, satellite monitoring, information regulation, e-navigation mechanism, and enhance joint preparedness to counter piracy, collisions, oil spills and other adversaries. Instead of completely substituting the Strait, it is viable for regional states to prioritise on enhancing infrastructural fragmentation and diversification through ports, railways, logistical corridors and alternative route development. Thailand’s 2026 decision to scale down the proposed Southern Land Bridge post its concerns over its commercial viability reinforces the need for an incremental and sustainable infrastructural planning. Furthermore, focus on capacity building and technological cooperation with other countries like India, Japan, China, and South Korea are also important while preserving the primacy of the littoral states. Altogether, strengthening the Strait requires infrastructural innovation, regional cooperation, and environmental preparedness alongside adaptive governance to determine the continuity of both trade and energy flows amidst changing geopolitical and economic situations.
