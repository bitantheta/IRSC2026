Bankipur, earlier known as Patna West, is a constituency which dates back to 1957, and has been represented at different points by the Congress, the Communist Party of India, the Janata Party, and even produced political figures such as former Bihar Chief Minister Krishna Ballabh Sahay and former Leader of the Opposition Sunil Mukherjee. The political character of the seat, however, changed decisively in 1995, when Nabin Kishore Prasad Sinha won Patna West for the BJP. He retained it through successive elections until his death in 2006, when his son Nitin Nabin entered the contest and won the bypoll by more than 65,000 votes. Following the 2008 delimitation, Patna West became Bankipur, but the BJP's hold remained intact: Nitin Nabin won the seat in 2010, 2015, 2020 and, most emphatically, in 2025, when he secured 98,299 votes, or 62.66% of the vote, defeating the RJD by 51,936 votes. By the time Nabin vacated the seat after his elevation to the BJP's national presidency, Bankipur had become a three-decade-old BJP fortress, built first by his father and then consolidated by him.

The pattern explains why Bankipur appeared to be the kind of constituency in which the BJP's organisational and social base could survive even the departure of its incumbent. This confidence became part of the BJP’s campaign itself. Prashant Kishor repeatedly attacked what he portrayed as the BJP's complacency in Bankipur, invoking a claim that the party believed it could win the seat even if it fielded a "dog or cat". Kishor used it to frame Bankipur as a test of whether three decades of uninterrupted BJP victories had produced a sense of entitlement among the party's leadership.

The 2025 result had given the BJP very little reason to expect such an upset. Nitin Nabin had polled 98,299 votes, with the RJD's Rekha Kumari on 46,363 and Jan Suraaj's Vandana Kumari on only 7,717. The BJP's vote share was 62.66%, compared with 29.55% for the RJD and 4.92% for Jan Suraaj.

The bypoll overturned almost every element of that arithmetic. Prashant Kishor secured 64,151 votes, Neeraj Kumar Sinha of the BJP received 44,827, and Rekha Kumari fell to 14,273. The Election Commission's final results show Kishor with 49.23% of the vote, Sinha with 34.40% and Kumari with 10.95%. Jan Suraaj therefore moved from 4.92% in the 2025 election to 49.23% in the bypoll, while the BJP fell from 62.66% to 34.40%.

The most striking feature is not simply that Kishor won, but also the scale at which the vote shares were overturned. Jan Suraaj had polled 7,717 votes in November 2025. Eight months later, it polled 64,151. The BJP lost 53,472 votes between the two elections, while the RJD lost 32,090.

The turnout of the two polls is, however, also an equally important factor. The bypoll recorded a turnout of 34.2%, compared to the 40.97% in the 2025 Assembly election. The lower turnout makes Bankipur particularly difficult to interpret as a straightforward measure of the entire electorate's preferences. A by-election attracts a more selective electorate, and parties must identify and mobilise voters rather than relying on the turnout generated by a full State election. That distinction may have mattered enormously in Bankipur because the constituency contains colleges, coaching centres and a large student population. Employment, examinations and education therefore had a direct local resonance. Kishor's campaign had spent considerable time presenting him as a candidate outside Bihar's established political system, and his message around jobs, governance and the perceived failure of established parties was designed for precisely the kind of aspirational urban electorate represented by Bankipur.

The political circumstances also favoured a candidate-centred contest. Nitin Nabin's departure removed the incumbent who had won 98,299 votes only months earlier. The BJP's first candidate, Abhishek Kumar, withdrew after filing his nomination, forcing the party to replace him with Neeraj Kumar Sinha. Nitin Nabin had been the face of the constituency since 2006. The BJP was now asking voters to transfer a mandate built around him to another candidate. Kishor, meanwhile, was the singular face of his campaign. This was particularly important because the BJP's Bankipur advantage had always rested on more than ideology. It involved a combination of the Nabin family's personal network, the BJP's organisational machinery, the constituency's upper-caste support base and the party's broader political appeal. The bypoll tested whether those elements were sufficient without Nitin Nabin's name on the ballot. The answer, at least for this election, was no.

The BJP was hardly passive. It treated the election as a high-stakes contest and mobilised a large number of senior leaders. Around 50 star campaigners were deployed in the constituency, while Nitin Nabin himself became deeply involved in the campaign to retain the seat he had represented for nearly two decades. The party's response showed that it understood the symbolic importance of Bankipur. Yet the intensity of that campaign may also have worked against its attempt to portray the election as merely a local contest. Once the BJP's senior leadership invested heavily in the seat, Kishor had greater room to present the bypoll as a test of the ruling party's wider record.

The BJP's own post-result response indicates that the defeat cannot be reduced to a simple transfer of RJD or Muslim votes. Nitin Nabin promised a "thorough introspection" after the result, while reports of the BJP's internal assessment pointed to dissatisfaction among sections of the party's traditional upper-caste support base, particularly younger voters. Kishor's campaign had raised questions of employment, education, examination systems and local governance, allowing him to reach voters who might normally have been expected to remain within the BJP's social coalition.

This is where Bankipur becomes politically more interesting than a conventional bypoll upset. Kishor did not merely have to attract voters who were already predisposed towards the Opposition. He had to make it electorally acceptable for voters in a BJP stronghold to abandon the party. His success suggests that the constituency contained a pool of voters whose partisan loyalty was weaker than the 2025 result had implied. The RJD's collapse reinforces this interpretation. The party's candidate had secured 46,363 votes in 2025. In the bypoll, that figure fell to 14,273. The RJD did not benefit from the BJP's loss. Instead, Jan Suraaj became the principal challenger and the RJD was pushed into third place. This matters because the RJD has traditionally occupied the central position in Bihar's Opposition politics. Bankipur demonstrates, at least in one urban constituency, that anti-BJP sentiment does not necessarily translate into an RJD vote. This also implies a more serious long-term question for the RJD than the loss of Bankipur itself. Kishor has spent the last several years presenting Jan Suraaj as an alternative to both the BJP and the RJD, rather than as another component of the existing Opposition alliance system. His victory now gives that argument an electoral example. A party that received only 3.34% of the vote statewide and won no seat in the 2025 Assembly election has, within months, produced an MLA by defeating the BJP in one of its strongest constituencies.

Another important factor that makes the Bankipur bypoll interesting is that it has challenged the long-held assumption that caste alone determines electoral behaviour in Bihar. The constituency has a sizeable upper-caste electorate, with Kayasthas constituting the largest single social group at around 14%. Other significant groups include Yadavs at 12%, Chandravanshis and Vaishyas at 9% each, Dalits and Bhumihars at 8% each, Brahmins at 7%, Rajputs and Kurmis at 5% each, and Kushwahas at 3%. Muslims account for another approximately 9% of the electorate.

The BJP's advantage was particularly pronounced among these upper-caste sections of the constituency. Kayasthas had traditionally formed an important component of the party's support base, reinforced by the fact that the BJP's candidates in Bankipur had consistently come from the community. Nabin Kishore Prasad Sinha and subsequently Nitin Nabin were able to combine this social base with the BJP's wider appeal among Bhumihars, Brahmins, Rajputs and other upper-caste voters. This was reflected in the scale of the party's victories: Nitin Nabin secured 62.66% of the vote in 2025, winning 98,299 votes and defeating the RJD by 51,936 votes. The BJP's support, therefore, was not simply a product of the Nabin family's personal influence, but was reinforced by a durable caste and organisational coalition. The 2026 bypoll disrupted that equation. Neeraj Kumar Sinha, despite being a Kayastha himself, received only 44,827 votes, a fall of 53,472 votes from the BJP's 2025 tally. The fact that the party could not transfer the support it had given Nitin Nabin to another Kayastha candidate is significant.

The RJD's traditional strength in Bihar rests heavily on the Muslim-Yadav communities, and both communities together account for a substantial share of Bankipur's electorate. Yet the party's performance in the bypoll indicates that this base was not sufficient to retain its position as the principal challenger to the BJP. Rekha Kumari, who had received 46,363 votes and 29.55% in the 2025 election, fell to just 14,273 votes and 10.95% in the bypoll. This makes the caste dimension of the result particularly important. Kishor did not simply consolidate the RJD's existing Muslim-Yadav support while benefiting from BJP abstention. The simultaneous collapse of the RJD vote and the BJP's loss of more than 53,000 votes suggests that Jan Suraaj attracted voters from across the constituency's established political alignments. The result therefore suggests that caste affiliation was mediated by candidate choice, party performance and the issues raised during the campaign.

The result should nevertheless be read within the specific conditions of the bypoll. Bankipur is an urban constituency with a distinctive social composition, and the turnout of the by-poll was substantially lower than in the 2025 Assembly election. Kishor's personal presence is another important variable. Jan Suraaj's performance here therefore demonstrates the electoral strength of Kishor as a candidate more clearly than it demonstrates the organisational strength of Jan Suraaj as a party. Whether the party can retain this support without Kishor personally leading the campaign remains an open question.

For the BJP, the defeat raises a more specific question about the durability of its traditional coalition in Bankipur. The party retained its organisational machinery and invested heavily in the campaign, but that was insufficient to reproduce the electoral advantage it had enjoyed under Nitin Nabin. The post-poll assessment reportedly pointed to dissatisfaction among sections of the party's traditional upper-caste support base, suggesting that the defeat cannot be reduced to the disruption caused by Nabin's departure alone. The BJP will now have to determine whether Bankipur was an isolated failure of candidate selection and mobilisation or an indication that sections of its established social base are becoming more receptive to issue-based and candidate-driven appeals.

For the RJD, the result raises a different problem. The party's difficulty was not simply that it failed to defeat the BJP. It was that another opposition formation displaced it as the principal beneficiary of dissatisfaction with the ruling party. Jan Suraaj's rise at the expense of both established parties suggests that Kishor was able to make himself a viable alternative rather than merely a protest candidate. This is particularly relevant in urban constituencies where traditional caste alignments can coexist with concerns over employment, education, governance and political representation.

Kishor's own response suggests that he intends to build on precisely this distinction. After the result, he rejected an alliance with the established parties, saying, "We shall have no alliance. Our alliance shall be with the people of Bihar." He also argued that voters had "given up on established parties" and that the result should serve as a message about the need for better leadership in Bihar, particularly on education and employment.

The question now shifts from whether Kishor can win an election to what he does with the mandate. He will have to govern Bankipur, retain the heterogeneous coalition that brought him victory and build a party structure capable of functioning beyond his personal campaign. The BJP will have to recover a constituency it had treated as secure, while the RJD will have to address the possibility that its position as the principal opposition vehicle is no longer uncontested. Bankipur, therefore, is best understood not as evidence of a statewide political realignment, but as a clear demonstration that the electoral space between the BJP and RJD is now capable of producing a viable third force in the form of the Jan Suraaj Party.
