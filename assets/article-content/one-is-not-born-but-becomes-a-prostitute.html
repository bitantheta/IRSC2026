In her article, “Wives and Whores: Revisiting Feminist Theorization on Sex Work”, Prabha Kotiswaran cites a paragraph from a Bengali Newspaper where Seema Nandy a housewife from Kolkata wrote the following letter and demanded a work licence for housewives. She made this demand to the mayor of Kolkata who once upon a time thought of institutionalising sex work.

“During the last thirty-three years of my married life, bereft of any recognition from the biological father and from the father-in-law, I could not understand throughout the whole of my life where do I belong, where is my home. Till date, I cry pretending that the tears were caused by smoke. Now if our Mayor considers the case of innumerable housewives like me, by conferring upon us the status of 'worker', then we shall be highly obliged. I am a housewife. I have pleased / entertained all the members and relatives of my in-laws, through domestic labour, for the last thirty-three years. I have tried to raise my children as good citizens. That apart, I had to do bone-crushing work day in and day out, while trying to divine the psychological conditions and needs of almost everyone. Even then there were no escape from numerous humiliations and insults. Parents told me that after marriage the house of the in-laws became my own home. Yet my husband-deity tells me on any slim pretext," I shall throw you out of the house"(Do mothers sometimes resort to female feticide because they swallow so much insult?) When the body revolts after the toils of a long working day from dawn to night, one has to endure the caresses of the husband, like a corpse enduring the beaks and claws of a swooping vulture. My request, sir, if you show a little sympathy towards these housemaids-cum-sex workers 'packaged as housewives', then at the end of our sleepless nights we shall no more be constrained to think that, 'at the end of the day, the sacrificial fire of dejection has turned everything into ashes'. My slogan on behalf of the housewives: 'We labour, that is why we eat, A license is what we need.”

- Seema Nandy, Kolkata 700 092”2

The complex interrelation between the institution of marriage and sex work broke many of my preconceived notions. At the same time, those very notions and understanding continued to search for differences between the two. One of the major difference lies in the legally recognized rights to reproduction and the varying degrees of legal and social acceptance of these two institutions. However, when Friedrich Engels wrote, “…monogamy and prostitution in the modern world, although opposites, are nevertheless inseparable opposite poles of the same social condition” this profound analysis is not only applicable for the contemporary period, but also to the ancient times.

Prostitution/Sex work was widely present in Ancient India. Many Hindus, Jain and Buddhist text freely discussed about prostitution at that time. But those texts and popular perception about sex work was very peculiar and maintain a double standard. In present times, sex workers are amongst the most marginalized people in our society, without any security and safeguards from state. But if we look back at the situation of sex worker in the ancient period, it was little paradoxical. Its emergence and recognition as a profession was presumably concomitant with the institution of strict marriage rules, especially monoandry, and the wife being regarded as the private property of her husband and sex worker regarded as the public woman (Samanya). Women who were unable to find any suitable husband, widows, unsatisfied married women (wife of Kulin Brahmin), raped women and many others forcibly were forced to or sometimes voluntarily, had to take up prostitution as a profession. They formed a social and economic group which was deeply monitored by the state. The body of the prostitute functioned not only as an object of male desire but also as a strategic and economic instrument for the state. This particular group was not a homogenous group, multiple hierarchies existed among them, shaped largely by the diverse demand of clientele. The social and economic expectations thus produce various categories of prostitution, for example: the ganikas and rupajivas belonged to two different classes, and their aesthetic sensibilities, intellectual abilities, remuneration, and other aspects differed significantly. Having a courtesan or a mistress outside of one’s marriage identified a man’s rich social status. Thus, women’s bodies worked as objects and symbols of one’s societal power. In ancient times, the state worked more closely with prostitutes, and used them as its strategic factor. This section of society gave a huge amount of tax to the state, The Nammayasundarīkathā, a twelfth century text says that the state received 25% to 30% of the prostitute’s income. In exchange of tax state gave security and protection. A complete state surveillance remained at that time which treated women’s bodies merely as an object to satisfy men’s desires and maintained a stability in society. Apart from economic benefits, the state also used them for their political purpose, employing them as spies. But the politics of family and marriage have always reduced the status of prostitute. From feudal to agrarian and eventually capitalist society, the economy has needed to sustain the monogamous family structure for its own market interest and patriarchy has work alongside to sustain it. The private woman was important for her reproductive value which ensured the continuous supply of workers. At the same time, male workers who were deeply dissatisfied with their wife due to perceived aesthetic and intellectual shortcomings, turned to brothel. Thus, the Samanya or public woman served the market by helping to maintain a “happy” workforce. Yet, the product these workers created remained utterly alienated from them. The society has always condemned the prostitute and never its clientele whose desires have sustained this trade to the present day. If one has to locate prostitution in Ancient India, it will be a tough task because they were deeply exploited but at the same time, they had some kind of autonomy over their work which was again surrounded by male centric surveillance of the state. If I say all sex worker at that time had some power, autonomy, and command over their work, it would be a wrong analysis to do, since the power and autonomy others factors highly depended upon one’s position in its hierarchy.

Another factor which creates multiple layers of hierarchies among sex workers was “caste system”. A year ago, during my internship period at RTF (Right to food Camping), I visited sonagachi (South Asia’s biggest Red Light Area) and I asked Mona Mishra, advisor of All India Network of Sex Work that “is there any kind of Caste based discrimination present among sex worker?”. In reply she told me “A Brahmin woman could never be a prostitute”. After reading Sukumari Bhattacharji and Sumanta Banerjee’s article on prostitute it is very clear to me that the factor of caste and racial beauty is deeply associated with caste which creates hierarchies. Many Kulin Brahmin wives and widow in ancient times had to choose or were forced to take prostitution as a profession. Not only that fishermen, craftsmen, chamar, lower caste workers could not even afford upper caste courtesans. After 19th century onward number of brahmin women in this field decreased significantly and lower caste Adivasi women joined this occupation more in numbers which lead a triple oppression in terms of class, caste and gender. Religion as a factor is another important institution which reshaped the institution of sex work. Though society took benefits from prostitution, it also condemned its work through many religious texts, one of them being Manusmriti. In the colonial times, we could also see a major difference in number between Muslim and Hindu prostitutes, when nearly half of the population was Muslim. Muslim women had the access to remarriage therefore their numbers were sharply less then Hindu women but many times, however, Muslim prostitutes had to use Hindu names to hide their identity for better work environment. Apart from that many religious rituals use the institution of prostitution as a drainage system or a graveyard by which every sin of men purified when a men go to brothel for example, in rural Bengali society, there existed a custom that the idol of Goddess Durga would remain incomplete unless some clay was taken from the doorstep of a prostitute’s house. The reasoning behind this curious belief was that the soil there was considered the purest, as it was thought to absorb the virtues abandoned by men who entered those doors. In this way, society cleverly transformed a social reality into a religious requirement, making the prostitute’s existence a part of sacred ritual. So, by many factors of society the political and societal location of prostitution changed many times.

Simone de Beauvoir in her book “Second Sex”, wrote a famous line “One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman.” Society creates and shapes a woman. In the same way No one is born a prostitute; one becomes one. The capitalist market economy depends on the family as a unit of reproduction and consumption, and therefore, it stigmatizes sex work. Through paid labour, a sex worker possesses a degree of economic agency that a housewife, confined within unpaid domestic work, often lacks. I repeatedly compare housewife and prostitution because to understand the position of sex worker in a society it is necessary to compare their social status. In India, the caste system perpetuates itself largely through the institution of marriage, which reinforces social boundaries and hierarchies. Therefore, every sacred value and high moral ideal associated with marriage technically exists to sustain certain social hierarchies. However, the institution of prostitution poses a threat to this supposedly sacred order. Paid sexual labour outside marriage inevitably raises questions about the unpaid domestic and reproductive work that wives have been performing for centuries. Hence, society systematically diminishes and stigmatizes the existence of prostitutes. Hence, the question raised by Seema Nandy is important.
