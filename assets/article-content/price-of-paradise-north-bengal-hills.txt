“Growth for the sake of growth is the ideology of the cancer cell.”

— Edward Abbey

The “Instagramification” of the hills of North Bengal is everywhere. Open Instagram and the hills appear almost impossibly perfect, the toy train winding through Darjeeling, green slopes disappearing into mist, Kanchenjunga glowing in the distance, steaming cups of coffee on chilly mornings, and photographs that seem to capture a slower, simpler life. Even the local strays become part of the aesthetic. The hills are beautiful, peaceful and almost untouched.

But what happens when a landscape becomes more visible than the people who live in it?

Beneath this carefully framed image lies a quieter crisis. The hills are becoming more connected, more visited and increasingly integrated into national and global markets. At the same time, they remain ecologically fragile, politically sensitive and strategically significant. Development is neither undesirable nor avoidable. Like every other region of India, the hills deserve roads, employment, healthcare, education, connectivity and economic opportunity. The question is not whether the hills should develop. The question is what kind of development is taking place, who benefits from it, and what is being lost along the way.

For many residents of Darjeeling–Kalimpong hills and adjoining Himalayan foothills of North Bengal landscape looks very different from the one presented to tourists. The quiet mornings of the hills once carried the sound of flowing springs. Today, many communities face seasonal water shortages. Research on Darjeeling has documented long-standing water scarcity and significant inequalities in access, despite the region's high rainfall.

An old resident in a remote Darjeeling village may remember a time when waking up meant hearing the sound of a nearby spring. Today, some of those sources have weakened or disappeared. When water moves farther away, the burden does not disappear; it is transferred to households often to women, who must spend additional time collecting it. Recent research from the Darjeeling Himalaya has documented women spending more than an hour collecting water from drying springs. This is not merely an environmental problem; it is a social one. It is an economic one. And increasingly, it is a political one.

## When Water Becomes a Commodity

The disappearance of springs is perhaps one of the clearest examples of how ecological change becomes a question of everyday inequality. Springs are not simply scenic features of the Himalayas. They sustain households, agriculture and local ecosystems. Recent research has warned that the drying of Himalayan springs has consequences extending beyond household water security, affecting agriculture, settlement and the resilience of strategically important mountain communities.

There is something deeply ironic about this crisis. In a landscape that appears abundantly green, water is becoming scarce. An elderly relative once warned my teacher as a child never to waste water. If people continued to misuse natural sources, she would say, there might come a day when they would have to buy water. The children laughed at the idea. Today, buying water is no longer an absurd possibility. It is an everyday reality for many locals. And this raises a larger question: where does the water go when the hills themselves appear to have so much of it?

Water does not simply follow gravity. It follows the path mediated by water-economics and water-politics nexus. In an unequal urban landscape, access to water is also shaped by income, infrastructure, institutions and power. Research on Darjeeling's water crisis has linked scarcity to population growth, the changing commercial character of the town and the pressures created by tourism, particularly during the driest months. Tourists may experience water scarcity as an inconvenience. For residents, it can determine how households’ function, how women spend their time and whether businesses can operate. The same landscape can therefore produce two entirely different experiences of water.

## When Local Abundance Becomes Market Dependence

The transformation is visible beyond water. In villages that appear surrounded by fertile green landscapes, households increasingly depend on products brought in from outside vegetables, packaged milk and other commercially produced food items. Local products and traditional food systems that once supported communities can become harder to sustain. This should not be romanticised. Traditional systems were not perfect, and markets can provide people with choices, convenience and livelihoods.

But when local production disappears without being replaced by equally viable local opportunities, something more than a dietary habit is lost. A livelihood disappears, knowledge disappears, relationship with the land disappears. Products such as ‘churpi’, a traditional Himalayan cheese, are not merely food items. They belong to a larger network of livestock rearing, local knowledge, household labour and cultural practice.

The issue, therefore, is not whether people should consume packaged milk or vegetables brought in from elsewhere. The issue is why communities living in a region capable of producing much of what they consume may increasingly depend on external supply chains. Calling this simply “modernisation” can conceal the inequality underneath it.

## Development Without Belonging

The hills have never been isolated from the outside world. Darjeeling itself was shaped by colonial economic structures, plantation labour, migration, tourism and political intervention. Its present identity is the result of layers of history rather than some untouched traditional past.

Yet development can become alienating when decisions about land, infrastructure and resources are made far away from the people who have to live with their consequences. This is where the politics of the hills becomes impossible to separate from their ecology.

The Gorkhaland movement, for instance, cannot be reduced to a simple demand for territory. Questions of political representation, economic marginalisation, language, cultural recognition and belonging have all shaped the politics of Darjeeling. For Indian Gorkhas and Nepali-speaking communities, questions of identity have also been complicated by the history of migration, citizenship and the persistent conflation of Nepali language or ancestry with foreignness.

The political question, then, is not simply: Who owns the hills? It is also: Who gets to decide what happens to them?

## The Hills Between Development and Dispossession

The rapid expansion of hill towns creates another contradiction. Tourism demands hotels. Growing populations demand housing. Connectivity demands roads. Businesses demand commercial space. Investors see land as an asset.

But mountains are not empty spaces waiting to be built upon. They are fragile geological and ecological systems. Unplanned construction, land-use transformation and deforestation can intensify environmental vulnerability. The deterioration of springs is also connected to broader changes in land use, climate and ecological management.

This is why the image of the “perfect hill station” can be misleading. The photograph captures the mountain. It does not capture the pressure beneath it. It does not capture the household waiting for water. It does not capture the young person who has left because there is no meaningful employment. It does not capture the family home occupied only by ageing parents. And it does not capture the political anxiety of communities that have spent decades asking what their place within the Indian political imagination should look like.

## The Young Are Leaving the Picture

Perhaps the most painful contradiction is demographic. The hills are increasingly attractive to outsiders while becoming increasingly difficult to sustain for many young residents. Tourists arrive for the landscape.

Investors arrive for property. Businesses arrive for opportunity. But young locals often leave for education, employment and a different future.

This creates an uncomfortable question:

What happens to a place when the people born there can no longer imagine their future there?

Migration itself is not necessarily failure. Young people should have the freedom to leave, explore and build lives elsewhere. The problem begins when leaving becomes the only realistic form of upward mobility.

A tourist may see a Kanchenjunga-facing apartment as an investment. A local young person may see the same apartment as evidence of a housing and employment economy increasingly disconnected from their own aspirations.

This is where class enters the picture. The hills are not inhabited by one homogeneous population. There are wealthy residents, tourism entrepreneurs, salaried households, workers, farmers and families struggling with basic services. Development therefore does not distribute its benefits equally.

The most visible transformation may be the new building on the hillside. The less visible transformation is the person who can no longer afford to remain there.

## From Picturesque Landscape to Strategic Frontier

The significance of the hills extends beyond tourism and local politics. North Bengal sits beside one of India's most strategically important geographical bottlenecks: the Siliguri Corridor, popularly known as the “Chicken's Neck.”

The corridor provides the principal road and rail connection between mainland India and the Northeastern Region. The Government of India has explicitly described the Siliguri Corridor as the route through which the mainland is connected to the Northeast.

Its importance extends beyond connectivity. The wider North Bengal region also connects India to neighbouring countries and important trade routes. This makes the region more than a tourist destination. It is simultaneously an ecological zone, a political space, an economic gateway and a strategic corridor.

That is precisely why its internal vulnerabilities matter. A strategically important region cannot be secure if the communities living within it are economically insecure, environmentally vulnerable and politically alienated.

Security is not only about soldiers, roads and borders. Sometimes, security begins with something as ordinary as whether a village has water.

Recent scholarship has argued that the drying of Himalayan springs can be understood as a non-traditional security concern because water loss can affect livelihoods, agriculture, migration and the viability of settlements in strategically sensitive areas.

The environment and national security, therefore, are not separate conversations. They intersect in the lives of people.

## What Will Remain When the Photograph Fades?

The hills do not need to be protected from development. They need to be protected from development that makes the region more prosperous in appearance while making its people more vulnerable in reality.

The answer is not to shut the hills away from tourism, markets or modern infrastructure. Nor is it to romanticise an imagined past in which everything was sustainable and harmonious.

The challenge is to make development compatible with ecological limits, local livelihoods and political belonging.

The hills need infrastructure but infrastructure that respects the terrain.

They need tourism but tourism that does not overwhelm local resources.

They need investment but investment that creates opportunities for residents rather than merely increasing property values.

They need connectivity but connectivity that allows young people to stay because they want to, not leave because they have to.

And above all, they need to be seen not merely as a beautiful frontier, but as a living region inhabited by people with memories, aspirations, grievances and claims to their own future.

Perhaps the most important question is therefore not how beautiful the hills will look twenty years from now. It is:

Who will still be able to call them home?

The next time a photograph of mist-covered Darjeeling appears on a screen, perhaps the question should not simply be, “How beautiful is this place?” Perhaps it should be:

“What does this picture leave outside the frame?”
