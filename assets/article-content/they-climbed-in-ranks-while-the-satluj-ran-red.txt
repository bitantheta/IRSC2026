“I challenge the Darkness. If nothing else then at least around myself, I will not let it settle. Around myself I will establish Light.” ~ Jaswant Singh Khalra

Satluj (2026) does not open with a hero. It opens with bodies. In the wicked dead of night, a police jeep stops, a few men are dragged out, and within seconds they’re executed. No warning. No explanation.  Just still silence after the gunshots. This was Punjab at the peak of the insurgency, when the men with badges stopped distinguishing the militants from civilians, and the night belonged to those holding the gun.

Who gets to decide which deaths count as history and which count as inconvenience? A state that once looked away while thousands of its people disappeared now insists that a film about those disappearances threatens its sovereignty.

Honey Trehan’s film Satluj follows Jaswant Singh Khalra, a Sikh human rights activist who investigated alleged illegal cremations and extrajudicial killings by the Punjab Police, and uncovered nearly 25,000 secret cremations of such. Khalra was then abducted and killed in 1995; a CBI inquiry and Supreme Court proceedings later convicted several Police officers.

Khalra's investigation did not only expose the secret cremation grounds, it also exposed what happened afterwards. As the Human Rights Watch observes, several officers accused of serious human right abuses went on to rise through the same ranks. Promotions came far quicker than any accountability. While the families searched for their disappeared loved ones, the system rewarded those accused of making them disappear. Their crimes were hidden, but their careers were not. Impunity became a policy.

None of this reached us audiences any easily. The film was set to release as Punjab’95 in 2023. However, the demands by the Censor Board reached to 127 cuts. The filmmakers refused, claiming that they had followed every legal requirement, and released the film uncut on ZEE5 as Satluj on July 3. Yet the Inter-Departmental Committee pulled it out within 48 hours anyway, citing threats to sovereignty and security concerns.

Supporters of the ban argue that the film threatens to revive buried separatist sentiments, and points to Khalra's alleged past links to secessionist editorial publications. Yet watching the film itself, a different picture emerges as Satluj barely utters the word Khalistan. It doesn't dwell on Operation Blue Star or the 1984 riots. Its narrative stays exactly where Khalra's own evidence stayed - on disappearances, cremation grounds, and paperwork that the state tries to pretend never existed.

Diljit Dosanjh, who played Jaswant Singh, never asked a single rupee for the role, telling Trehan that charging money to play a martyr would be shameful: which hints the film was never about the paycheck.

Despite the ban, the film keeps finding its way to the people it's made for as it is community screened in various Sikh temples across Punjab, widely downloaded and shared, and watched anyway. Meanwhile, other politically charged films, which seems somewhat favourable to those currently in power, haven't just been left untouched on the same platforms but some have been actively championed, and are backed with tax breaks, special screenings, and in some cases leave for government employees to watch them.

Khalra's only wish was to give the disappeared ones their names back, to match a cremation-ground receipt to a family still waiting at their doors. Yet, the same forces abducted him from his home, beat him, then shot him. His body was driven to Harike, the exact point where the Beas meets the Satluj, and thrown into the water. The men who did it were rewarded with liquor with their seniors celebrating. He has never been found. The investigator became one more entry in his own ledger, except now no one was there to match his name to the waiting family. Some of the men responsible climbed through the ranks for a decade before a conviction finally caught up to them. The man who counted the dead joined the count instead: now uncounted. The Satluj did not choose any sides. It just kept moving, carrying what the state wanted to be gone, until it ran red.

If sovereignty is truly at risk, why does it need protection from this one story only, and never from the others? How can the files of a dead bank employee-turned-activist threaten the sovereignty of a nation - when the killings documented in the files didn’t? The government never released an official statement either explaining the ban, or giving the director a single stated reason for those 127 cuts. Thirty years on, it seems Jaswant Singh Khalra's voice was still enough of a threat that the state had to silence it again this time, in 2026. Yet even now, far across the land, in some small hut, a lantern still hasn't gone out.
