‘Mone Kori Assam Jaabo’, a folk song of great renown, widely available across the internet, has become exemplary of a curious predicament owing to the contrast between lyrics such as

“Babu bole kaam-kaam,
Sardar bole dhori aan,
Shaheb bole libo pither chaam,
O Joduraam, phaki diya pothaili Assam”

and the commonly made artistic choices in its presentation. Although critics to the approach abound, one YouTube search for the song yields dozens upon dozens of iterations of the same paradox- exuberant musical arrangements and dance choreographies entirely incongruous with the sombre history the song represents.

The earliest verifiable modern scholarly printing of this particular edition of the song is in Rana P. Behal's One Hundred Years of Servitude, but it originated as a part of an oral composite of the tea-garden ‘Jhumur’ repertoire, born of the exploitative ‘Arkathi’ and ‘Sardari’ systems that carried Adivasi labour from Chotanagpur and its adjoining regions to the Assam plantations. The scale was vast: while according to the Journal of Namibian Studies, 22,800 tea labourers migrated to Assam in 1867–68 and 76,041 in 1880 alone, Behal's book recorded that recruiters transported over three million contract workers into Assam between 1860 and 1947. The language of the song is itself evidence- Sadri and Bhojpuri-inflected constructions from the recruiting districts meet Bengali, the lingua franca of the plantation belt and of the Surma valley, and Assamese vocabulary picked up in the gardens. The result is a phrase like ‘boga boga phul’ sitting inside an otherwise Bengali line. No village in Chotanagpur produced that mixture, the tea gardens did. The song then, is a plantation artefact created by a population assembled from many communities compelled to fabricate common speech.

Commercial tea growing in Assam began in the late 1830s with the Assam Company- the industry's labour problem began almost immediately. Assamese peasants had land and had little reason to accept plantation rigour, and local labouring groups such as the ‘Kacharis’ could bargain. The planters' answer was to import workers from outside the province and to designate them as ‘coolies', a category that carried several legal disabilities. From the 1860s the traffic was systematised. Recruitment ran through two channels: the Arkathi, a professional contractor's agent working on commission, and later in an attempt at reform, the Sardar, a serving worker sent back to his home district to recruit kin and neighbours. Transit itself killed: overland marches to river depots at Goalpara and Dhubri, then steamers up the Brahmaputra, with mortality high enough to be a recurring subject in official correspondence.

The legal architecture surrounding these recruitments points at the literal implications of the punishment indicated by the lines “Shaheb bole libo pither chaam”. The Inland Emigration Act of 1882 and its successor Act VI of 1901 governed the indentures, and their central instrument was the penal contract; breach was a criminal offence, and planters held a right of private arrest over absconders. Alongside these ran the Workmen's Breach of Contract Act XIII of 1859 and section 492 of the Indian Penal Code, under which refusal to work could be punished with imprisonment. When the penal clauses of Act VI of 1901 were abolished by amendment in 1908, planters shifted their new engagements onto Act XIII of 1859, which was in several respects harsher. The Assam Labour Enquiry Committee of 1921 to 1922 recorded men and women sentenced to rigorous imprisonment and hard labour for periods ranging from 3 weeks to 3 months for refusing to comply with contract provisions, and reported children arrested and held pending trial.

The stanza quoted earlier is perhaps the most historically weighted in the song. It features three figures of plantation authority speaking in turn- the babu demanding work ("babu bole kaam kaam"), the Sardar ordering defaulters seized ("Sardar bole dhori aan"), and the Saheb threatening to take the skin off the workers' backs ("Saheb bole libo pither chaam") - closing on the refrain that curses the recruiter Jaduram for deceitfully sending the woman to Assam ("phanki diya pothaili/cholaili Assam"). Alternative interpretations assume that Joduram refers to a lover who has smuggled the singer into the tea garden under false promises of marriage, as indicated by the line “Jora ponkha tongaibo”, alluding to the aspiration for a peaceful conjugal life. The assignment of the three demands to the three authorities, notably men, is scrambled from version to version, which is itself diagnostic of oral transmission rather than a single authored text. The song then serves as documentation of the uniquely marginalised position of Adivasi women specifically, who were vulnerable to exploitation at once by all these figures, and usually came from socially and economically precarious backgrounds.

The song's second life can perhaps be traced back to the IPTA (Indian People’s Theatre Association) generation in Bengal and Assam, and Hemanga Biswas in particular, who took the tea-garden repertoire seriously as political material and wrote about it comparatively across Bengal and Assam. Kali Dasgupta, who spent 1965 onwards in England in the orbit of folk musicians Ewan MacColl and Peggy Seeger before returning to enrich the tradition in India, tape-recorded "Chol Mini Assam jabo" in the Assam gardens in the late 1960s and early 1970s. Sleeve notes accompanying the music he published from the gardens state plainly that the singer curses Jadhuram, the agent, for bringing these individuals to Assam under false pretences. The song eventually entered the urban Bengali folk revival which led to the introduction of the genre to an audience with no idea of its provenance. The consequence of this is that a Chotanagpuri Adivasi song has become, for most Bengali listeners, a Bengali folk song, paired frequently with a cheerful acoustic guitar in Kolkata.

While there is a case to be made for freedom of expression and the entitlement to individualised interpretation, there is perhaps simultaneously a line to be drawn between self-expression and an expression that honours and understands the original authorial intent of the text. Manufacturing joy in the pursuit of music production is undeniably potent and politically effective, besides being a revered, essential social tradition- however, when the distance between the performance of a song majorly passed down orally and its textual content grows so large, the risk is run of a practise far more sinister taking root. For literature of any form being passed down orally, those partaking in that continuum must also indulge in the essential ethics of preservation. Donna Haraway argues that all knowledge is partial and ‘situated’ in that it is shaped by the specific historical, social, and geographical context of the observer. Similarly, all art engaged with is also done in a situated manner- when the question is that of preservation of an oral composition belonging to an exploited community, considerations of power and hierarchy must necessarily assume paramountcy.
