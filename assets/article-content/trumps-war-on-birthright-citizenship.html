When one goes down, another appears up. This is the principle of the game of whack-a-mole, and now, is the principle of President Trump’s attempts to repeal birthright citizenship. After his initial attempt was rejected by the courts, his new orders made more subtle attempts to indirectly repeal it. On January 20, 2025, Executive Order 14160 was issued, which sought to adopt a more limited approach to stripping birthright citizenship from children born on US soil to mothers present there unlawfully or temporarily and fathers who were neither citizens nor legal residents. This had violated the Fourteenth Amendment’s Citizenship Clause of the United States (US) Constitution, 8 U.S.C. §1401(a) and the US Supreme Court’s decision in Wong Kim Ark (1898). On June 30, 2026, the Supreme Court, in Trump v. Barbara, ruled children born to unlawful or temporary residents as citizens but limited remedies due to injunctions. This marked a firm rebuke of Trump’s exploitation of his presidential powers to bypass Congress, and significantly narrowed the scope of actions that the lower courts could take in response to his orders.

Undeterred, on August 6, President Trump issued two new orders, “Continuing to Protect the Meaning and Value of American Citizenship and “Ending Birth Tourism” - restricting foreign government officials, alien enemies, and foreign terrorist organisations, and imposing more restrictive visa and entry rules on birth tourism, respectively. Fraud is not irrelevant to the issue of birthright citizenship, but conflating exceptions to jus soli with opportunities to erode its theoretical foundation is committing a slippery slope fallacy in reverse. It is one thing if the government starts to take active steps to prevent fraud, but it is another thing if exceptions are built into the system on other grounds. One way this could happen is, by using visa policy to circumvent the clear wording of the Constitution. Restrictions on visas and documentation may undermine birth tourism and may allow for a gradual erosion of birthright citizenship.

Nonetheless, the implications of these orders go far beyond immigration. Citizenship based on the jus soli principle, as opposed to jus sanguinis, was firmly established by the Constitution. Given this context, where is the US actually heading to? Is American citizenship going to be based on birthplace or parentage? This remains a controversial issue. The children cannot be held responsible for the actions of their parents, so punishing them amounts to a kind of collective penalty. For instance, millions of Indian immigrants and their US-born children, alongside the H-1B, L-1, F-1, and visitor-visa families find themselves in an uncertain crisis. With 1.2 million Indians waiting for green cards and a 7% cap on employment-based visas under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) §202, lawful presence will lead to a chain reaction of analogous problems and permanent questions about the citizenship of a vast majority of America’s future generation.

Amending the Constitution is a complex process that requires the support of more than two-thirds of Congress and three-fourths of the states. Therefore, the courts have the responsibility to oppose birth tourism in other ways. The Congress must also take measures to combat “visa trafficking,” while federal agencies must issue standard identity documents to children. An executive order can be repealed with another executive order, while amending the Constitution is significantly difficult. Thus, it is crucial to emphasise that the central issue here is not whether Trump is going to win another round in the whack-a-mole game, but whether the US is going to roll back to the next stage in the dismantling of its constitutional democracy. Above all, the grandson of a German immigrant should remember - a nation that disowns its immigrant soul disowns itself.
