Introduction

In June 2026, the newly formed BJP government in West Bengal announced that the International Society for Krishna Consciousness (ISKCON), through its Annamrita Foundation, would prepare and supply cooked mid-day meals under the PM-POSHAN scheme in schools under the Kolkata Municipal Corporation as a pilot project (Times of India, 2026; The Hindu, 2026a). The material cost per student was raised from Rs 6.78 to Rs 10 (The Hindu, 2026b). ISKCON, the organisation that follows a strict ‘sattvik’ diet which meant that the school children would be served with a vegetarian diet that would exclude eggs, onions and garlic. Critics questioned the imposition of vegetarianism on a population where an everyday food basket consists of an overwhelmingly non-vegetarian diet. The backlash prompted Chief Minister Suvendu Adhikary to clarify that eggs would not be entirely removed from the menu; the children would be provided with eggs through a parallel channel of SHGs (Millennium Post, 2026; The Hindu, 2026c).

On plain observation, this entire overhaul might appear to be a routine bureaucratic decision-making- division of work between ISKCON and SHGs- but deep beneath, two major questions reappear- a tough question on the parameters of a ‘pure’ food, and also how a publicly funded nutritional programme adapts to the dietary practices of a religious organisation?

A State That Eats- and the Data That Proves It

National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5, 2019-21) data clearly show that more than 90 per cent of the population aged 15-49 in West Bengal consumes fish, meat, chicken or eggs on a daily (IIPS & ICF, 2021). The same survey also highlighted that the state spends a greater share of their household food budget on fish and meat than states like Gujarat, Haryana, or Rajasthan (AsiaNews, 2026). The consumption actually rose between NHFS-4 and NHFS-5, moving in the reverse direction of any push towards vegetarianism. The state records among the lowest rates of vegetarianism across major Indian states, cutting across caste and religious lines. The most popular item among the dishes revolves around fish- something which is associated beyond dietary practices, used in religious and cultural practices such as bhog offered to Ma Durga every year.

Then there is the question of nutrition. Eggs are considered one of the most cost-effective sources of complete protein accessible to India’s public nutrition programmes (Hindu Business Line, 2026; Business Today, 2026). Eliminating or reducing egg supply- even partially can increase the cost of health in the state, since West Bengal continues to battle high rates of child malnutrition and anaemia even in urban centres like Kolkata (AsiaNews, 2026; The Wire, 2026).

Having a simple understanding of the food habits of the state, question does obviously arises: when an organisation whose philosophical framework considers non-veg items like meat, fish and eggs as tamasic or impure in nature is entrusted with the responsibility of running a universal, state-funded nutrition scheme for children, does it not represent an imposition of minority dietary ideology onto a population for whom the diet is of different nature? Moreover, the organisation does not have a clean record in delivering its duty: its food arm, Akshaya Patra Foundation, came under the scrutiny of Karnataka State Food Commission for disappearance of onion and garlic from Patra’s mid-day meals (The Hindu, 2019a; The Hindu, 2019b); Chandigarh authority decided to cancel their deal with the organisation, citing mismatch in the local dietary preference of students (SabrangIndia, 2019; The Citizen, 2017).

Purity, Pollution, and the Sociology of the Plate

M. N. Srinivas, writing on caste mobility in mid-20th century India, coined the term “Sanskritisation” (in his book ‘Religion and Society Among the Coorgs of South India’) to describe the phenomenon where lower caste groups in a region changes its original customs, food habits, culture and way of life following the footsteps of the twice-born (dwija) caste (Srinivas, 1952). Srinivas pointed out how the lower caste people even adopted vegetarianism and teetotalism in an effort to elevate their ritual purity- acts historically associated with Brahmanical status. The process of Sanskritisation does not end the hierarchy; it only allows for repositioning in the ladder, keeping the principle of purity and pollution intact (still considers meat eating as a polluting activity). Now, with a state apparatus adhering to Brahminical sattvik food code, the Sanskritisation is adopted not as a process of social aspirations but as a force policy design – where upper caste food choices are presented as a default setting, while the overwhelming fish- and meat-eating population is the deviant, the “impure” in its own home.

Historicising the political economy of food habits, B. R. Ambedkar, in an analysis of the origin of untouchability, argues that the Brahminical community adopted strict vegetarianism as a strategic response to outmaneuver Buddhist opposition by appealing to superior non-violence. In this process, beef eating, which was once common among Hindus, became the symbol of the ‘broken man’ (later considered untouchables) (Ambedkar, 1948). In Ambedkar’s understanding, the food taboos were not a continuation of spiritual ideals but tools that shaped and codified graded inequality. When a controversial religious sect that treats eggs as pollution is given charge of feeding children of all castes and communities in a secular setting, the old belief of classification is quietly reintroduced in the name of hygiene or nutrition.

Conclusion

Food is never only about calories or nutrition. In India, it has long been a language of hierarchy, resistance and identity. Therefore, the controversy surrounding the mid-day meal is beyond a mere single pilot project. It asks whether public policy will continue to treat the purity-pollution framework as a voluntary religious preference that can be imported into state schemes, or whether the Republic will insist that children’s nutritional rights remain free of such status. The separate provision of eggs is a necessary remedy; the deeper corrective would be a policy design that does not begin by accommodating one organisation’s dietary theology at the expense of the lived food cultures of many.
