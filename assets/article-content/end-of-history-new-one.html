India’s strategic autonomy is most convincing when it is paired with a clear programme for fairer, more representative global governance.

Introduction: a world beyond the “end of history”

In 1989, Francis Fukuyama argued in “The End of History?” that Western liberal democracy might represent the endpoint of humanity’s ideological evolution. The decades since have unsettled that proposition. Democratic backsliding, authoritarian nationalism, economic inequality and great-power rivalry have made the international order more contested, not less.

For India, this unsettled order creates both opportunity and responsibility. Its foreign policy has long emphasised strategic autonomy: the ability to make choices on the basis of national interest rather than formal alliance commitments. During the Cold War, that instinct found expression in non-alignment; after the Soviet Union’s collapse, it helped India widen its diplomatic, economic and security partnerships. Today, however, strategic autonomy must mean more than balancing among powerful states. It must also help India advance an international order in which the Global South has meaningful influence.

Why multilateralism needs reform

Multilateral institutions remain essential for dealing with climate change, pandemics, debt distress, cyber insecurity and armed conflict. Yet the principal institutions of global governance still reflect the distribution of power at the end of the Second World War. The UN Security Council’s permanent-membership structure and the weighted voting systems of the International Monetary Fund and World Bank give disproportionate influence to a small group of wealthy states.

Decolonising multilateralism does not mean rejecting cooperation or replacing one hierarchy with another. It means making institutions more representative, transparent and responsive to the countries most affected by global decisions. That includes stronger representation for Africa, Latin America and South Asia; a more equitable voice in international financial institutions; and a greater role for developing countries in setting rules on trade, development, health and climate finance.

India has repeatedly presented itself as a voice for the Global South. Its case will be strongest when that language is matched by sustained coalition-building and a willingness to share responsibility. Reform is not only an argument about status; it is an argument about whether institutions can still command legitimacy in a multipolar world.

Strategic autonomy in a multipolar order

India’s contemporary diplomacy is better described as multi-alignment than as the old policy of non-alignment. New Delhi works with the United States and European partners on technology, investment and maritime security; participates with China and Russia in forums such as BRICS and the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation; and continues to rely on its long-standing defence and energy relationship with Russia. These relationships serve different interests and cannot be reduced to a single bloc affiliation.

The logic is clear. Cooperation with Western partners can support access to capital, critical technologies and resilient supply chains, while the Quad provides a forum for consultation in the Indo-Pacific. Engagement with Russia preserves a durable strategic relationship and helps diversify defence and energy options. Dialogue with China in multilateral settings does not remove the border dispute or the strategic asymmetry between the two countries, but it keeps channels of communication open where they are necessary.

This approach carries risks. The United States and China are both vital economic partners for India, even as their rivalry intensifies. China’s military posture on the border directly affects India’s security, while Russia’s growing isolation has made balancing more difficult. Strategic autonomy, therefore, is not a posture of detachment. It requires difficult choices, credible capabilities and a clear explanation of the principles that guide those choices.

The test of consistency: Gaza and West Asia

West Asia illustrates the tension between India’s normative language and its strategic partnerships. At the inaugural India–GCC Joint Ministerial Meeting for Strategic Dialogue in Riyadh in September 2024, External Affairs Minister S. Jaishankar expressed concern over civilian deaths in Gaza, called for a ceasefire and reiterated India’s support for a two-state solution. India has also continued to affirm support for Palestinian statehood in official statements.

At the same time, India’s ties with Israel have deepened, particularly in defence, technology, agriculture and innovation. Prime Minister Narendra Modi’s February 2026 visit to Israel underscored the breadth of this partnership and the countries’ intention to expand cooperation. India’s position is thus neither a simple break with its past nor an uncomplicated continuation of it: New Delhi seeks to retain its stated support for a negotiated Palestinian state while building a close strategic relationship with Israel.

That balancing act invites legitimate scrutiny. If India wants to speak credibly for a fairer global order, it must apply international law, humanitarian protection and civilian security as consistently as possible, including when its strategic partners are involved. The question is not whether India should abandon relationships; it is whether its diplomacy can articulate and defend principles that remain credible across cases.

From aspiration to institutional leadership

India’s growing economic and diplomatic weight raises expectations that it will help shoulder global burdens. A serious programme for multilateral reform could include support for an expanded and more representative Security Council, governance changes at the IMF and World Bank, stronger South–South cooperation, and climate and health arrangements that recognise differentiated capacities and responsibilities.

Such an agenda would also require practical diplomacy: building durable partnerships with African, Latin American, Asian and small-island states; contributing to development finance and humanitarian responses; and using forums such as the G20, BRICS and the UN to translate broad principles into negotiated outcomes. Representation without delivery will not be enough.

Conclusion

The old multilateral order is under strain, but no settled replacement has yet emerged. India is well placed to influence that transition because it can speak across several diplomatic groupings and because its own history gives it standing in debates on sovereignty, development and postcolonial equality. Strategic autonomy can be more than a method for managing risk. If coupled with consistent principles and concrete institutional reform, it can become a contribution to a more inclusive international order—one in which Global South voices help shape the agenda rather than merely respond to it.
