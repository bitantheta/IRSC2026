For over six decades, the Indus Water Treaty (IWT) of 1960 stood as a singular monument to functionalist diplomacy, a conflict-resilient agreement that survived three full-scale wars and numerous border skirmishes between India and Pakistan. However, the landscape of South-Asian hydro-politics underwent a terminal ‘Phase Shift’ in 2025. Following the Pahalgam terror attack in April 2025, New Delhi placed the IWT in ‘abeyance’, citing national security concerns and Pakistan’s state-sponsored terrorism. This move, coupled with the completion of the Shahpur Kandi Dam in March 2026, signals a fundamental transition in India’s water diplomacy, a move from passive adherence to a doctrine of maximalist utilization and strategic legal statecraft. Linkages that have developed between water security and national security have added a new facet to the competitive interaction between India and Pakistan. As India weaponizes its geographical position as an upper riparian power, it is not merely securing its water resources, it is an attempt of recalibrating its strategic autonomy and projecting power across the Indo-Pacific.

The ‘Zombie’ Treaty: India’s Pivot towards Hydrological Realism

The Indus Water Treaty, signed on September 19, 1960, by Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru and President Ayub Khan, is widely regarded as one of the most successful transboundary agreements globally. Brokered by the World Bank, it partitioned the Indus system’s six rivers. The Eastern Rivers – Ravi, Beas, and Sutlej were allocated for India’s exclusive use, while the Western Rivers – Indus, Jhelum, and Chenab, were primarily for Pakistan, with India permitting limited “non-compulsive” use for irrigation and hydropower. For six decades, the IWT survived three full-scale wars, serving as a rare conflict-resilient pillar of stability.

The suspension of the Indus Water Treaty in 2025 marks a hydrological quake that has effectively turned the treaty into a ‘Zombie State’. Historically, India failed to utilize its full entitlement of the Eastern rivers, due to significant infrastructure deficits. This resulted in a passive dividend for Pakistan, where the surplus water flowed downstream to sustain the Lahore basin and nearly 80% of its agricultural land. The completion of the Shahpur Kandi barrage in March, 2026, represents the kinetic manifestation of the “blood and water cannot flow together” doctrine. By blocking the final drainage point of the Ravi River, India is now diverting 1,150 cusecs to irrigate over 32,000 hectares in Jammu and Kashmir’s Kathua and Samba districts.

However, this shift is as much legal as it is physical. As per several political analysts, India’s decision to place the treaty in abeyance rather than outright termination is a calculated move in strategic legal statecraft. By invoking the principles of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT), specifically Article 60 (Material Breach) and Article 62 (Fundamental Change of Circumstances), India argues that sustained cross-border terrorism has altered the very basis of the 1960 agreement. Critics and legal scholars note that while the IWT lacks a unilateral exit clause under Article XII, New Delhi is leveraging customary international law to justify its pause. This abeyance serves as a non-forcible counter-measure, using law as a leverage to induce a change in Pakistan’s security policy. This sovereign consolidation ensures that water is no longer a ‘unearned gift’ to a hostile neighbour but a tool for domestic development and strategic leverage.

India has started to work on three hydropower projects in Jammu and Kashmir, entailing a combined investment of around $3 billion, as the government looks to use excess water domestically for electricity generation instead of letting it flow into Pakistan. India is pushing for speedy development of hydropower projects on the Chenab with a combined capacity of over 5,000 megawatts and renegotiation of the six-decade-old pact. India's position on the Indus Waters Treaty rests firmly on the treaty's provisions and its right to use the western rivers for any approved purpose, including electricity generation. New Delhi has stated that projects such as the Kishenganga and Ratle hydroelectric projects adhere to the treaty's provisions and are crucial to address the growing developmental needs of Jammu and Kashmir. For India, the Indus Waters Treaty dictates how the shared water must be used.

On the other hand, India has expressed concern over Pakistan's growing tendency to pursue disputes—such as the one over the Kishenganga and Ratle hydroelectric projects—through a Court of Arbitration, arguing that this bypasses the treaty's prescribed, graded dispute resolution mechanism. According to India, technical disputes between the two countries should instead be handled through the treaty's own institutional frameworks, especially the Permanent Indus Commission. According to sources, the government has assessed that the pact has outlived its utility and is ready to renegotiate it with Pakistan to make it more balanced. For any future reactivation of the river water-sharing provisions, the treaty would need to be renegotiated in a modified format — a step that remains contingent on Islamabad permanently halting its support for terrorism directed against India, sources said. Any future agreement, if negotiated, would reflect today’s realities including – climate change, glacier retreat, shifting hydrology, rising demand, new technology, and security concerns.

Meanwhile, India says it is pressing ahead with full utilisation of Indus waters. Two major projects, the 624 MW Kiru and 1,000 MW Pakal Dul hydroelectric plants, are due to be commissioned this year. India plans to add another 5,500 MW in Jammu and Kashmir over the coming years, including the 1,856 MW Sawalkot, 850 MW Ratle, 240 MW Uri 1&2, 260MW Dulhasti St 2 and 540 MW Kwar projects. Many of these were long opposed by Pakistan. These projects are critical for India’s clean energy transition, grid stability and regional development, and will substantially enhance India’s hydropower capacity while enabling the fuller utilisation of its legitimate rights under the Indus basin, said sources.

Together with accelerated hydropower development and modern basin management, these initiatives will ensure that India’s legitimate share of the Indus waters is utilised efficiently, sustainably and in accordance with its long-term developmental, water security and clean energy objectives. The impending completion of the Ratle and Pakal Dul projects on the Chenab River by 2027-28 will further grant India absolute control over the timing of water releases, creating what many analysts have called ‘Water Vacuum’ for Pakistan’s agricultural stability.

Beyond Farakka: Crafting a Climate-Resilient Blueprint for the 2026 Renewal

While the Western front is defined by confrontation, the Eastern front is governed by the Ganges Water-Sharing Treaty, signed in 1996. Valid for 30 years, India’s diplomacy in the East faces an existential challenge: the expiration of the Ganges Water-Sharing Treaty in December 2026. It provides a fixed formula for sharing the flows of the Ganges at the Farakka Barrage during the lean season (January to May). Unlike the IWT, which divides the rivers, the Ganges treaty shares the actual volume of water, making it highly sensitive to seasonal variations and climate change.

In the wake of political volatility in Bangladesh following the political exit of Sheikh Hasina, the necessity of water cooperation remains a pragmatic anchor for regional stability. The 1996 treaty is increasingly viewed as an outdated relic that fails to account for the ecological and environmental consequences of water sharing in a climate-stressed era. For India to maintain its strategic lead without descending into a diplomatic deadlock, the 2026 renewal must transition towards environmental peacebuilding. This requires a planetary health makeover that treats river basins as single entities, incorporating climate resilience and technological data-sharing into the core of the agreement. By shifting the narrative from volume-based sharing to benefit-based management, India can secure its eastern flank while positioning itself as a leader in climate-sensitive diplomacy.

Hydro-Gateways: Rivers as the Sinews of the Indo-Pacific

In the traditional geopolitical imagination, the Indo-Pacific is a maritime construct, defined by blue-water navies and deep-sea ports. However, under India’s recalibrated “Act East” policy, the strategic map is being redrawn to include its riverine arteries. No longer viewed merely as domestic resources or sources of border friction, the rivers of North-east India, specifically the Brahmaputra, Barak, and Kaladan are being transformed into ‘Hydro-Gateways’. These waterways serve as the ‘sinews’ or connective tissues that bind India’s landlocked hinterlands to the maritime commerce of the Bay of Bengal and the wider Indo-Pacific.

The revitalization of North-east India’s river networks is the cornerstone of this strategy. By developing National Waterway-2 (NW-2) on the 891 km stretch of the Brahmaputra and National Waterway-16 (NW-16) on the Barak, New Delhi is shifting the region from a geopolitical periphery to a ‘logistical fulcrum’. The inauguration of integrated customs and immigration complexes at Bogibeel and Dhubri in February 2026 signals a move toward high-efficiency, multi-modal logistics. These developments, on one hand provide a sustainable Indo-Pacific gateway for commerce and, on the other, offer a vital alternative to the vulnerable Siliguri Corridor, or ‘Chicken’s Neck’. By leveraging the Indo-Bangladesh Protocol (IBP) routes, India is ensuring that its economic security is no longer hostage to a single, narrow terrestrial link.

The most ambitious manifestation of this ‘sinew’ strategy is the Kaladan Multi-Modal Transit Transport Project (KMTTP). Designed to connect the eastern port of Kolkata to the Sittwe Port in Myanmar, the project transitions into a riverine corridor along the Kaladan River to Paletwa, eventually linking by road to Mizoram. Despite security challenges in Myanmar’s Rakhine state, the project slated for full operationality by 2027, represents a paradigm shift in the regional integration. It bypasses traditional bottlenecks and integrates the Bay of Bengal into a seamless transit network, effectively making the Kaladan a strategic ‘river-sea’ bridge to South-east Asia. By treating the Bay of Bengal as a shared economic zone, India is using its riverine infrastructure to foster a Hydro-solidarity that counters the fragmentation of regional trade.

This outreach, however, is shadowed by the Double Riparian reality. While India acts as a dominant upper riparian towards Pakistan and Bangladesh, it remains vulnerable to China’s hydrological hegemony on the Yarlung Tsangpo (Brahmaputra). China’s planned 60 GW ‘Super-dam’ at the Great Bend is not just an environmental threat but also a hydro-digital flashpoint where control over water mirrors control over regional data and energy grids. India’s response has been a $77 billion hydropower initiative, including the 11 GW Upper Siang project, aimed at establishing prior usage rights and building counter-capacity. This is where the concept of ‘Sovereign Consolidation’ becomes kinetic. By asserting physical control over the Brahmaputra’s flows, India is moving away from passive diplomacy towards a model of active deterrence.

The transition from passive adherence to a Physical Control model reflects a broader national strategy of using every available lever, hydrological, legal, and economic, to secure India’s interests. On the global stage, this shift signals the rise of India as a “Hydro-Hegemon” that is increasingly willing to bypass multilateral mediation. By rejecting the competence of the Permanent Court of Arbitration and the World Bank’s traditional roles in the Indus dispute, New Delhi is asserting a form of Strategic Autonomy that prioritizes national security over 20th-century rules-based orders. This maximalist stance has profound global implications. India is moving away from the functionalist hope that water can be a bridge to peace, adopting instead a realist view where water is the primary currency of power. If India can successfully manage its eastern treaties through climate-sensitive diplomacy while maintaining its abeyance leverage in the west, it will set a new global precedent for how rising powers manage transboundary resources. Ultimately, India’s water diplomacy is no longer just about sharing cusecs, it is about the Sovereign Consolidation of its geography. In a 21st-century Indo-Pacific defined by resource scarcity and climate volatility, the ability to command the flow of rivers is synonymous with the power to shape the regional order. As a rising hydro-hegemon, India’s challenge will be to ensure that its quest for strategic autonomy does not lead to a regional “water vacuum” but rather to a new, more resilient framework for “Cooperative Hydro-Diplomacy” that can withstand the storms of the Third Nuclear Era.
